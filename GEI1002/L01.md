# Introduction: Concepts of Data & the Humanities

## Ethnography vs. Data Science

- **Ethnographers** immerse themselves in communities, observe behaviours, conduct interviews, and analyse cultural practices.
- **Data scientists** use computers to discern trends that might be inaccessible to the naked eye.

Numbers and stories are intertwined: hard data, which might seem objective at first glance, is based on human decisions; and data can guide more qualitative inquiries.

### Challenges in the age of AI

- Bias in training data can lead to biased AI decisions, perpetuating or even amplifying existing societal prejudices.
- There is a risk of overreliance on quantitative data, potentially missing important qualitative factors crucial to understanding human behaviour and preferences.

The goal is to combine insights from the humanities and data science — to interpret numbers and stories together, and to critically analyze data while never losing sight of the context behind the numbers.

# Part I: Tidy Data and Decision Diagrams

## Two definitions of data

- **Data science perspective:** a collection of measurements, attributes, or observations that can be analyzed to derive insights or make decisions.
- **Humanities perspective:** a representation of human experiences, behaviors, and cultural artifacts — contextual and interpretive in nature.
- Both definitions can be true at the same time.

## Types of data

- **Quantitative:** numerical data that can be measured and analyzed mathematically.
- **Categorical:** data that can be divided into groups or categories.
- **Ordinal:** categorical data with a natural order or ranking.
- **Continuous vs. Discrete:** quantitative data that can take any value within a range, vs. data with distinct, separate values.

## Tidy data

Principles:

- Each variable (or feature) forms a column.
- Each observation forms a row.
- You can't combine different types of data in the same column (e.g., "4.7 stars" mixed with "4.5"; "$18" mixed with "16–35").
- There should be only one observation per row. Wide formats like `Sales_Day1, Quantity_Day1, Sales_Day2...` should be reshaped into long format with a `Day` column so each row is a single observation.

## Objectivity and subjectivity in data

Data can be **subjective** or **objective**. A guiding question: which type of variable tends to be more subjective — numerical or categorical?

## Complexities in data collection and representation

Real-world situations resist tidy structure. For example:

- What if a restaurant has two addresses — do we list both?
- What if a restaurant moved — how do we note that?
- For "Years Open," what if it was closed for 6 months?
- What if a place kept the same name and address but changed its entire menu?

## Schematic bias

- **Dataset schema:** the structural blueprint of a dataset. It specifies the variables, observations, and constraints that govern the structure and content of the dataset.
- **Schematic bias:** systematic distortion or limitation in data collection and representation that arises when complex realities are forced to fit predetermined categories or structures.

It manifests in two primary ways:

1. In the **initial design** of data collection — decisions about what to include and exclude shape the potential insights and limitations of the dataset.
2. In the **processing** of data — where information is trimmed, transformed, or transfigured to match the expectations of the database schema or analytical tools.

It reflects the tension between the need for standardized, machine-readable data and the messy, context-dependent nature of real-world information. There is no way to completely avoid schematic bias; recognizing it is crucial for understanding the limitations of datasets and blind spots in data-driven decision making. When creating a dataset, deciding what to include means some things will be left out.

## Proxies

Data is always an imperfect approximation of reality, dependent on available technology and conscious choices made by those designing the schema and collecting the data. This is described as a **proxy** — sometimes data is a good proxy for the phenomenon studied, sometimes not.

- **Good proxy example:** using food centre stall turnover rates as a proxy for hawker industry health.
- **Poor proxy example:** using the number of void deck events as a proxy for community engagement.

## Cultural context in data collection

Decisions about data collection are informed by the worldviews, histories, values, and priorities of those collecting it. These might seem odd to those looking from outside or another context.

## The decision diagram

A tool for analyzing existing data projects and planning new ones. It maps the sequential choices behind a dataset. Example dimensions for a "pizza dataset":

- **Pizza definition:** only serves pizza / 50% of menu is pizza / at least one pizza on the menu.
- **Address definition:** single address only / multiple addresses allowed.
- **Data collection method:** collect from websites / systematically visit all pizza places in an area.
- **Data collection area:** the entire city / only one district.

## Definitions matter

Even the basic unit of analysis requires a decision. What exactly counts as a "pizza place"? Only places that exclusively serve pizza? Places where 50% of the menu is pizza? Places with just one pizza on the menu? There is no perfect answer — you must make a decision and clearly communicate it to those who might use the data.

## Data provenance and unintentional errors

- When analyzing a dataset, also note **who created the data and why**.
- Be aware of **unintentional errors** (e.g., the 2020 case where Excel's row limit caused UK COVID-19 test results to be lost).
- Data reporting has limitations — e.g., COVID-19 vaccination trackers where figures exceed 99%, some values are estimates or outdated, and small-population countries are excluded.

# Part III: Identifying Limitations and Omissions

## "Biography" of a dataset

Questions to interrogate a dataset's history:

- **Origin:** Who created the dataset and why?
- **Context:** What was happening in the world when the data was collected?
- **Evolution:** How has the dataset changed over time?
- **Challenges:** What obstacles were encountered during data collection?
- **Unexpected turns:** Were there surprising discoveries or changes in direction?

### Example: WHO Global Tuberculosis Database

- **Origin:** The WHO began systematically collecting global TB data in 1995, recognizing the need for comprehensive, standardized information to combat the disease.
- **Context:** Initiated during growing concern about HIV/AIDS and its interaction with TB, and the emergence of multi-drug resistant TB strains.
- **Evolution:**
    - 1995: Initial focus on case notifications and treatment outcomes.
    - 2000: Expanded to include TB/HIV co-infection data.
    - 2006: Incorporated data on multi-drug resistant TB (MDR-TB).
    - 2010: Added financial data on TB program budgets and expenditures.
    - 2015: Began tracking progress towards the UN Sustainable Development Goals.
    - 2020: Included data on the impact of COVID-19 on TB services.

## Close reading a dataset

- Always look at the raw data.
- Read as many rows as possible, not just the first few.
- Look for patterns, outliers, and inconsistencies.
- Pay attention to how missing data is represented.

### Example: ImageNet

ImageNet is a large visual database (>14 million hand-annotated images) designed for visual object recognition research, used to train many early machine learning image-recognition models. In 2019, artists Trevor Paglen and Kate Crawford close-read the person categories (via the project "ImageNet Roulette"), finding:

- **Offensive labels:** many person categories included slurs, racist terms, and derogatory labels.
- **Subjective judgments:** categories based on appearance or perceived character traits (e.g., "bad person," "snob," "failure").
- **Gendered stereotypes:** certain professions were unnecessarily gendered.
- **Pseudoscientific categories:** classifications based on discredited theories such as phrenology.
- **Privacy concerns:** individuals labeled with highly personal or sensitive characteristics without consent.
- **Inconsistent granularity:** some categories were extremely specific, others broad and vague.

Consequences: ImageNet's creators removed 600,000 images of people, eliminated many problematic person categories, and the case sparked broader discussion in the AI community about the ethics of training data.

## Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

**Summary statistics:**

- Central tendency (mean, median, mode)
- Dispersion (standard deviation, range)

**Exploratory visualizations:**

- Histograms to show distribution
- Box plots to identify outliers
- Scatter plots to show relationships between variables

EDA can also help identify errors (e.g., a time series where cases suddenly drop to zero, signalling a reporting break rather than a real change).

## Getting a feel for the data

Combine both traditions:

- **From ethnography:** paying attention to context, emphasizing history, focusing on definitions, appreciating nuance.
- **From data science:** systematic analysis, computational tools, exploratory data analysis and visualization.

A good understanding of data comes from understanding its origins and context, looking at individual data points, examining summary statistics, and exploring visualizations.
