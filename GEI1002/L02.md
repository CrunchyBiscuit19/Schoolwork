# What is Data?

## Learning objectives

- To define data.
- To consider the relationship between data and interpretation.

## Data and interpretation

Data does not speak for itself — using data means making decisions, and those decisions are interpretations.

- **The publishing industry example:** A New York Times graphic (based on Richard Jean So's _Redlining Culture_) shows that just 11% of fiction books published in 2018 were written by people of colour, while 89% were written by white writers. Producing such a graph requires deciding what data to collect (author race across thousands of books from a handful of major publishers).
- **Treating _The Lord of the Rings_ as data:** The novels can be turned into a dataset — e.g., counting lines of dialogue per character, then visualizing them as a bar chart or pie chart.

In both examples, the designers of the visualizations made decisions. These decisions reflect what they think is important, and alternative decisions would have led to very different visualizations.

## Organizing data in spreadsheets

In this module we only use spreadsheets to organize data:

- Each **row** is an object.
- Each **column** holds data for one **feature** of that object.

# Part 2.1: Defining Data

## The DIKW pyramid

Data sits at the base of the **Data–Information–Knowledge–Wisdom (DIKW) pyramid**. One way to frame it: **data is potential information**.

> "Where is the wisdom we have lost in knowledge? Where is the knowledge we have lost in information?" — T.S. Eliot, _The Rock_ (1934)

## Using data: input → process → answer

Data is treated as an **input** that is fed through a **sequence of steps** (a process) to produce an **answer** (an output).

## Is a novel data?

- Imagine a digital file containing all of Tolkien's novels. If you simply **read** them on your computer, you are **not** treating them as data — you are treating them as **information**.
- To treat the novels **as data**, you need to process them systematically (e.g., extracting and counting lines of dialogue per character) so they become an input to analysis that produces an answer such as a visualization.

## Context: "when are data?"

- Data is **context-dependent**.
- The question should not be "what are data" but **"when are data"** (Borgman, 2015).
- That which is data in one context might be information or knowledge in another.

## Types of data

Data can take many forms:

- Free text
- Categories
- Coordinates
- Floating point numbers
- Etc.

These can be **manually assigned** or **computationally derived**.

**Every category is a preconception** — but it is very hard not to impose categories.

## Characteristics of a dataset (Manovich, 2020)

1. A phenomenon is represented as a set of **objects** (also called data points, measurements, samples, records) and their **features** (also called attributes, characteristics, metadata, variables). Features may include already-available metadata as well as measurements generated using algorithms (a process called **feature extraction**).
2. Together, the objects and their features form a **dataset**.
3. The number of objects in a dataset has to be **finite**.
4. Features are encoded using **data types**: whole and fractional numbers, categories, spatial coordinates, spatial shapes and trajectories, dates, times, text tags, or free text.
5. Each feature can use **only one data type**.
6. The number of features in the dataset has to be **finite**.

## Different objectives across disciplines

Different disciplines use data with different goals:

- **Prediction** (Science)
- **Control** (Engineering)
- **Explanation** (Social science)
- **Interpretation** (Humanities)
- **Automation** (Machine learning)

## Data everywhere

- Data has changed our understanding of nature.
- We now have access to many sources of data in digital format, which makes analysis easier.
- But this raises a question: does this mean that **every aspect of our lives should be decided by data**?

# Part 2.3: Problems of Using Data

## The proxy problem

Sometimes we don't have data for the exact thing we want to measure, so we choose to measure something else — a **proxy**.

**Example — grading by proxy:** Imagine grading students in this course using data from their earlier courses, assuming past performance predicts future performance.

Potential issues:

- It **locks you into the past** (what if you genuinely tried harder this semester?).
- The system would be **biased towards people who had a great start** (for whatever reason).
- Worse, using data on "students similar to you" (based on zip code, gender, chosen major) to assign scores introduces discrimination.

**A cultural example:** "Films have become more innovative over time." How do you decide if a film is more innovative? A potential proxy answer: the number of keywords. But this may be a poor proxy for the concept of "innovation."

**Why proxies are dangerous:** It is very tempting for businesses and institutions to use data this way — loans, job applications, health insurance, prison sentencing.

### Baseball vs. Weapons of Math Destruction (Cathy O'Neil)

- Areas where data makes sense are like **data-driven baseball** (as in _Moneyball_): baseball has statistical rigor, an immense dataset, and its data is **highly relevant to the outcomes it tries to predict** (balls, strikes, hits).
- By contrast, the builders of **"Weapons of Math Destruction"** routinely lack data for the behaviors they care about, so they **substitute stand-in data, or proxies** — drawing statistical correlations between a person's zip code or language patterns and their potential to repay a loan or handle a job.
- These correlations are **discriminatory, and some are illegal**. Baseball models mostly avoid proxies because they use pertinent inputs (O'Neil, _Weapons of Math Destruction_, 17–18).

# Part 2.4: Data in the Humanities

## Data about culture

Sources of humanities/cultural data include:

- Digitized books, films, artworks, video recordings
- Motion capture
- 3D models
- Surveys
- Production details (film, theatre, books)
- Historical records (posters, sales data)
- Social media (objects shared, but also interactions)

## Limited data in the humanities

Humanities data is often incomplete because much of it is lost or not digitized. For British nineteenth-century novels (Schöch):

- **20,000–30,000 novels** published
- **6,000 novels** still extant
- **3,500 novels** available in full text

## Big data vs. smart data

Two contrasting types of data:

- **Big data**
    - Characterized by **volume, variety, velocity** (and sometimes **veracity and value**).
    - Outliers and errors are **less important** (sheer scale compensates).
- **Smart data**
    - **Markup, annotations, and metadata**.
    - **Clear data models**.

**The future of data for the humanities (Schöch, 2013):** The goal is to move towards data that is both **increasingly big** and **increasingly smart** — combining the scale of big data with the structure and richness of smart data.

## Objectivity and interpretation

- **No data in the humanities is fully objective.**
- It requires active efforts to collect or produce.
- It depends on:
    - What **technology** enables.
    - The **opinions and perspectives** of the people creating a system.

## Problems of data

Key problems to watch for (including in group projects):

- **Standardization**
- **Incompleteness**
- **Inaccuracy**
